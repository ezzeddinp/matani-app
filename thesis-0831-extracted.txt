INTRODUCTION`nIndonesia is an agrarian country with a population of more than 270 million people and a continuing need to maintain national food security. Agriculture, forestry, and fishing contributed approximately 12.5% of Indonesia's gross domestic product in 2023, while employment in agriculture accounted for about 28.8% of total employment [1], [2]. Rice (Oryza sativa L.) remains the country's primary staple crop. According to BPS-Statistics Indonesia, Indonesia's paddy harvested area in 2023 reached about 10.20 million hectares, producing approximately 53.63 million tons of dry unhusked paddy [3].`nDespite its importance, rice farming in Indonesia still faces several operational challenges. Climate variability has made rainfall patterns and temperature conditions less predictable, affecting irrigation availability and increasing exposure to drought, excessive rainfall, heat stress, and inundation. At the same time, many farmers have limited access to accurate and location-specific information about crop condition, water stress, and local weather risk. As a result, field management decisions are often based on experience alone rather than timely data.`nRemote sensing offers a practical way to observe rice fields at broad spatial coverage without requiring continuous ground measurement. Multispectral satellite data can be processed into vegetation and moisture indices that describe crop greenness, canopy development, and water-related stress. NDVI is widely used to monitor vegetation vigor, while SWIR-based indicators, such as moisture-related indices derived from near-infrared and SWIR bands, can provide additional information related to crop water content and soil moisture conditions [4], [5]. When combined with weather data, these indicators can support early identification of field-level risk patterns.`nThis study presents Matani as a padi-only mobile decision support prototype for inspecting explicitly state-labeled satellite-index, weather, and field-note observations over time. The product displays dated raw NDVI/NDMI values when available and an explicit unavailable state otherwise. It does not classify crop health, diagnose disease or stress, predict yield, or synthesize missing observations. The paper-defined GROW workflow frames Gathering available inputs, Recognizing observable changes without causal interpretation, Offering educational context or optional descriptive summaries, and Warning through transparent weather-context indicators; thesis-target monitoring depends on the quality-gated measurement pipeline.                  `nThe long-term goal of Matani is to support a more resilient rice-farming workflow by making field context and uncertainty easier to inspect. Any contribution to improved farm management, poverty reduction, or food security remains a future impact hypothesis; those outcomes were not evaluated in this study.`nII. LITERATURE REVIEW`nA. NDVI, SWIR, and Remote Sensing for Rice`nRemote sensing has been widely used for rice field mapping, phenology monitoring, and crop condition assessment. Zhao et al. reviewed satellite-based rice mapping methods and highlighted the importance of multi-temporal optical, microwave, and multisource remote sensing approaches for paddy rice monitoring [4]. Kuenzer and Knauer also discussed remote sensing methods for rice crop areas, showing that vegetation indices and temporal image patterns are central to rice field analysis [6].`nNDVI is one of the most established vegetation indices for describing crop greenness, canopy development, and biomass-related variation. Guan et al. demonstrated that high-resolution UAV-derived NDVI was related to fertilizer application levels and yield variation in rice and wheat fields [7]. Jiang et al. further showed that UAV operation parameters, including flight altitude and acquisition timing, influence NDVI quality in paddy rice fields [8]. Although UAV imagery can provide very high spatial resolution, its limited coverage and operational cost make it less suitable as the only monitoring layer for scattered smallholder fields.`nSatellite-derived NDVI is also useful for broader-scale monitoring. Huang et al. showed that multi-temporal NDVI derived from satellite data can support rice yield trend analysis and reflect seasonal vegetation dynamics [9]. However, NDVI primarily captures structural vegetation conditions and may be less sensitive to early water stress or moisture variation. For this reason, SWIR-based indicators are relevant because SWIR reflectance is commonly used in vegetation water-content and soil-moisture assessment. Recent reviews of Sentinel-based soil moisture assessment show that Sentinel-1 radar data and Sentinel-2 multispectral indices, including moisture-related indices such as NDMI, are frequently integrated with machine learning to improve agricultural water monitoring [5].`nThese studies indicate that NDVI is appropriate for structural crop condition monitoring, while SWIR-derived moisture indicators can complement NDVI by adding water-stress information. Therefore, a mobile decision support system for rice farming should avoid relying on a single index and should instead combine vegetation, moisture, and weather indicators in a form that is understandable for farmers.`nB. Machine Learning for Rice Monitoring and Decision Support`nMachine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) techniques have been widely applied to rice yield estimation and crop monitoring using satellite and multi-source data. Cao et al. integrated satellite imagery, weather variables, and management data to predict rice yield across large regions in China [10]. Son et al. developed ML models using time-series MODIS NDVI data for rice yield prediction in Taiwan [11]. Jeong et al. combined crop growth models and deep learning to predict rice yield at pixel scale using satellite data in South and North Korea [12]. Son et al. also used Sentinel-2 monthly image composites with ML algorithms for field-scale rice yield estimation [13].`nBeyond yield estimation, ML has been used to classify rice production stages and analyze vegetation dynamics. Thorp and Drajat applied deep learning with Sentinel-1 and Sentinel-2 data to map paddy rice production stages across West Java, Indonesia, using extensive ground survey data [14]. Their study demonstrates the relevance of combining satellite data and machine learning for rice monitoring in the Indonesian context.`nHowever, many yield-prediction and anomaly-detection studies depend on historical outcomes, detailed ground truth, and extensive calibration datasets. Such data are unavailable for the present Matani evaluation. Machine-learning-based crop-health inference, anomaly attribution, growth-stage classification, and risk prediction are therefore outside the implemented and evaluated scope.`nC. Environmental Stress Monitoring and Rice Phenology`nVegetation indices can be used to detect abnormal growth patterns associated with environmental stress in paddy rice. Ryu et al. evaluated vegetation indices under elevated air temperature, heat stress, and herbicide damage, showing that NDVI can represent structural crop development and help detect abnormal growth conditions before the heading stage [15]. Their findings also indicate that different vegetation indices respond differently to structural, biochemical, and physiological stress conditions.`nFurther research by Ryu et al. showed that NDVI and the Photochemical Reflectance Index (PRI) respond differently to heat stress damage in paddy rice at leaf and canopy scales [16]. These findings suggest that NDVI is useful for monitoring crop structure and development, while additional indices may be required to capture early physiological stress. For scalable mobile applications using open-access satellite data, NDVI can serve as the primary structural indicator, while SWIR-derived moisture indicators and weather variables can improve the interpretation of stress risk.`nTime-series inspection can add value because a single vegetation-index value does not describe temporal change. Matani's current detail surface reads dated legacy fixture/prototype NDVI and NDMI rows without generating replacement dates or values and without interpreting their direction as improvement, deterioration, or diagnosis. Provenance-safe measured history remains dependent on the target persistence pipeline.`nD. Weather-Based Risk Indicators`nRice cultivation is highly sensitive to climatic variability, particularly during critical growth periods. Short-term extreme events such as heatwaves, excessive rainfall, drought periods, and prolonged inundation can reduce yield and weaken crop condition even before severe visual damage appears. Compound dry and wet extremes during the rice-growing season have been shown to increase the risk of rice yield loss more strongly than individual dry or wet extremes [17].`nClimate risk indicators are therefore important for rice farming decision support. Chou et al. analyzed extreme climate events in rice-growing regions and used indicators such as warm days, consecutive dry days, maximum five-day precipitation, and rice heat damage indices to describe climate-related risk [18]. Jiang et al. further examined heat exposure risk for single-season rice, showing the relevance of disaster-event-based heat risk assessment for rice production [19].`nIn the current Matani prototype, weather retrieval provides precipitation and humidity inputs to a client-side heuristic score, defined later in Section III.I. The score is a rule-based presentation-layer indicator, not a calibrated agronomic model. More complex indicators such as consecutive dry days, heatwave duration, or inundation risk remain future extensions unless they are implemented, versioned, and validated.`n`nE. Research Gap`nAlthough previous studies have demonstrated the value of multispectral satellite data, vegetation indices, SWIR-derived moisture indicators, and machine learning for rice monitoring, several gaps remain in operational farmer-facing decision support systems.`nFirst, local sensor-based systems, such as Internet of Things (IoT) devices and ground-based sensors, can provide high-resolution field information, but their cost, maintenance requirements, data storage needs, and rural network limitations can reduce adoption among smallholder farmers. A recent systematic review of IoT and AI in agriculture identified high infrastructure costs, limited interoperability, connectivity constraints, and unequal access as major barriers to implementation [20]. Low-cost remote sensing approaches have been explored for smallholder agriculture, but UAV-based systems still face spatial coverage and operational constraints [21].3090263709213`nSecond, traditional monitoring methods based on manual field surveys require substantial time, labor, and financial resources, particularly for national-scale coverage across millions of hectares of rice fields[4]. Many ML-based yield prediction studies also require extensive historical yield records and detailed ground truth data, which are not always available in smallholder farming contexts[10]. This creates a need for decision support approaches that can work with accessible satellite and weather data while still communicating uncertainty clearly.`nThird, the operational integration of weather data, dated NDVI/NDMI observations, and field notes into a transparent mobile time series remains underdeveloped [5]. Many systems present weather separately, while remote-sensing studies often require expert interpretation. There is a need for interfaces that expose observation dates, missingness, and provenance limits without converting index values into crop-health diagnoses.`nMatani addresses this gap by combining dated NDVI and SWIR-derived NDMI observations, weather context, field notes, and optional descriptive AI summaries within a mobile time-series interface. Health classification, diagnosis, anomaly attribution, and prediction remain outside the evaluated implementation.`nThe measurement evaluation is guided by two active research questions; an independent raster-comparison question is retained only as optional future work:`nRQ1: Within the approved padi study field in Teluknaga, Banten, what proportion of Sentinel-2 acquisitions produces quality-passing polygon-level NDVI and NDMI observations, and what monitoring cadence is defensible during the locally labelled wet and dry seasons?`nOptional future work: How closely do pipeline-derived polygon statistics agree with independently derived raster zonal statistics for the same polygon, source product, acquisition time, bands, mask, and grid policy? This comparison is not an active thesis acceptance requirement.`nRQ3: For the approved Teluknaga padi field, how many valid NDVI and NDMI pixels are available per acquisition under the provisional quality floors, and what measurement limitations arise from the field's size and Sentinel-2 spatial resolution?`nIII. METHODOLOGY`nA. Research Design`nThis study employs a system-development and decision-support research approach to design Matani as a padi-only mobile application for rice-field time-series monitoring. The active measurement evaluation is a one-field, one-site retrospective case study over the advisor-approved Teluknaga padi boundary and its locally labelled wet and dry seasons. The application and research remain observation-first: Matani displays dated raw NDVI/NDMI values and explicit data states, but does not provide crop-health classes, diagnosis, exact yield prediction, or soil-fertility prediction. The provisional valid-pixel floors classify observations for this study only, while the client-side weather-context score remains author-defined and uncalibrated.         `nB. Weather-Context Heuristic`nThe prototype employs a heuristic scoring model developed in this study to translate selected weather variables into simple risk levels for farmer-facing decision support. Precipitation and relative humidity are selected as inputs because rice cultivation is sensitive to water-related extremes, including insufficient rainfall, excessive rainfall, and wet atmospheric conditions that may increase field monitoring needs [17]-[19].`n`nThe heuristic prototype formula is defined as:`nRisk Score = min(100, max(0, (Pavg × 4) + (H × 0.4)))`nwhere Pavg is the average daily precipitation in millimeters over the forecast period, and H is the representative relative humidity value obtained from current weather data when available. The coefficients 4 and 0.4 are heuristic weights defined in the prototype to emphasize precipitation as the dominant short-term water-related risk factor, while humidity provides secondary modulation.`nThis score is not adopted from an established agronomic risk model. It is an author-defined prototype indicator calculated in the mobile presentation layer and is not calibrated against local field observations. Its value is therefore shown as a heuristic score rather than a probability of crop loss.`nTABLE IV`nRisk Level`nScore Range`nAdvisory Interpretation`nLow`n0 – 39`nFavorable or low-risk short-term weather condition`nMedium`n40 – 69`nElevated risk, increased monitoring advised`nHigh`n70 – 100`nHigh heuristic indicator; field verification required`n`nThis scoring model is explicitly positioned as a prototype heuristic decision support indicator, not a validated agronomic prediction model.
